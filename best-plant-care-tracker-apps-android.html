Most plant tracker apps are not tracking anything.

The Play Store bundles three different products under "plant app": identifiers, subscription care planners, and actual plant trackers. Picking the wrong category is why people install four and keep none.

01 · THE CATEGORIES

"Plant app" covers three products that barely overlap.

Before comparing features, work out which of these three you are actually shopping for. Nearly every disappointing install is a category error.

Identifiers — "what is this plant?"

Photo-recognition apps such as PictureThis and PlantNet answer a one-off question: name the species, sometimes diagnose a problem. They are online-first by necessity — the model runs on a server — and they are usually the ones with the aggressive subscription prompt, because the inference costs money every time you press the shutter.

Good at: naming an unlabelled plant, spotting a pest. Bad at: remembering that the fern by the north window has been snoozed three weeks running. Identification is a lookup, not a record.

Care planners — "tell me what to do"

Apps such as Planta and Vera build on a curated species database and generate a care plan from it: watering intervals adjusted for pot size, light level and season, plus rich guidance per species. Typically subscription-funded, because maintaining that database is real ongoing work.

Good at: beginners who do not yet know what a plant needs and want a confident default. Bad at: the moment your judgment diverges from the plan. And the plan usually lives behind an account.

Trackers — "remember what I did"

Trackers assume you already know roughly what your plants need and solve the memory problem instead: which plant, in which room, due for what, and what happened last time. This is the category most people mean by plant tracking, and it is the smallest of the three, because it is the hardest to monetise.

Good at: collections past a handful of plants, people who like their own judgment, anyone who wants the data on the device. Bad at: telling a beginner what a Calathea wants. It will not.

Store listings change. Features and pricing in this space move quickly. Verify the current listing on Google Play before you commit — this page describes what each category is built to do, which is stable.

02 · CRITERIA

Five questions that actually separate plant trackers.

  • Can one plant have several care schedules? Watering, feeding, misting, rotating and repotting run on different rhythms. Apps that collapse them into one "reminder" make it impossible to know what is due.
  • Can you snooze and skip, not just complete? The correct answer to a watering reminder is often "not today." An app with only a Done button teaches you to lie to it.
  • Does location mean anything? Two plants in the same room can sit in completely different light. Grouping by real conditions — east window, shaded corner — beats grouping by room name.
  • Is the history visible and useful? If you have deferred the same reminder four times, the interval is wrong. Only an app that shows you that will let you fix it.
  • Does it work with the phone offline, and is your data exportable? Reminders and a plant list have no reason to need a server.

03 · THE DECISION

Pick by what you are bad at, not by what the app is good at.

The honest question: is your problem knowledge, or memory?

  • You do not know what your plants need → a care planner. Pay the subscription while you are learning; the curated intervals are worth it in year one.
  • You have unlabelled plants → an identifier, used once, then uninstalled or kept for emergencies. It is a reference book, not a habit.
  • You know what to do and keep forgetting to do it → a tracker. This is most people after their first year, and the point at which subscriptions start feeling like rent on your own notes.
  • You have more than about eight plants → a tracker, regardless of the above. Past that count, the care history becomes more valuable than any reminder.

Where Mossby sits: firmly in the third category. It organises plants into zones and pots, keeps watering, fertilising, misting, rotating and repotting on separate schedules, shows the day's due and overdue care in one list, and warns before a plant lands in a room that is too dark or shares a pot with something incompatible. It works offline and needs no account. It will not tell you what your plant is, and it has no curated species encyclopaedia — if that is what you need, one of the planners above is the better buy.

04 · IN PRACTICE

The setup most collectors end up with is two apps.

An identifier for the rare question, a tracker for the daily one. That pairing costs nothing ongoing and covers both jobs properly, because each app is doing the thing it was actually built for.

The failure mode worth avoiding is the opposite: a single subscription app used as all three, where the care plan is authoritative, the record is thin, and cancelling the subscription takes your plant history with it. Ask what happens to your data on the day you stop paying — that answer is the real comparison.

This page is about choosing software, not horticulture. Follow the care needs of the actual plant in front of you and adjust when its condition says the schedule is wrong.

05 · QUICK ANSWERS

Plant tracking apps, briefly.

What is a plant tracker app?

It records the plants you own, where each one lives, the recurring care each needs, and what you actually did. Identification and diagnosis are a separate job handled by different apps.

Do I need a subscription to track my houseplants?

No. Recurring reminders, a care history and room organisation are ordinary local features. Subscriptions typically pay for photo identification or a curated species database.

Should a plant watering app work offline?

For tracking, yes. Reminders, care history and your plant list have no reason to need a connection. Photo identification genuinely does, which is why identifiers are online-first.

How many plants before an app is worth it?

Roughly five to eight. Below that a phone reminder is enough. Above it, the care history becomes more valuable than the reminder itself.
